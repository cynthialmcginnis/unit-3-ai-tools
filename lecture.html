Eight out of ten

Companies and governments spend billions of dollars on artificial intelligence (AI) projects every year. An AI project is the work of building or buying an AI system and putting it to use.

The most common reason AI projects fail:

More than 8 of every 10 AI projects fail. That is twice the failure rate of technology projects that do not involve AI.

In 2024, researchers at the RAND Corporation, a research organization that studies policy for the U.S. government, interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers about why AI projects fall apart. The most common answer was not bad code or a weak model. It was leaders who misunderstood, or explained badly, what problem the AI was supposed to solve.

Tonight is about everything around the model: choosing the right problem, planning the work, testing the tools, and keeping the system honest after launch.

Why AI projects fail

RAND found five root causes behind most failed AI projects. Almost none of them are about the model.

Read them as a checklist. Any recommendation you write should show you checked all five.

Section 1 · Root causes

Five root causes

Root causeWhat it looks likeIn a scenario
1. The wrong problemLeaders misunderstand or explain badly what the AI is supposed to do. The team builds something accurate that nobody needs.The clinic asks for no-show predictions, but its real problem is that patients cannot get through on the phone to reschedule.
2. Not enough good dataThe data needed to train the model does not exist, is too messy, or does not match the real problem.The school has reading test scores twice a year, too rarely to catch a student falling behind in October.
3. Chasing the technologyThe team picks the newest tool because it is exciting, not because it fits.The bakery is sold a GenAI chatbot when a simple sales forecast would do the job.
4. Weak infrastructureThe organization lacks the systems to move data, run the model, and keep it updated.The flight line's maintenance records sit in three systems that do not connect.
5. A problem too hard for AIThe task needs judgment or information AI does not have yet.Predicting a rare part failure that has happened only twice in ten years.

The first cause is the most common and the cheapest to prevent. Executives tend to overestimate AI. In a 2022 Gartner survey, 80 percent of executives said automation could be applied to any business decision. That belief produces expensive projects aimed at the wrong target.

"Not enough good data" does not mean you need a huge amount of data. The word that matters is good: data that is accurate and matches the real problem. A small set of good data beats a mountain of messy data that answers the wrong question. How much is enough depends on the problem. We cover that in Unit 4.
Section 1 · Preventing root cause 1

Name the problem before you choose any technology

Root cause 1 is the wrong problem: the team builds something that works, and nobody needs it. You prevent it by naming the problem precisely, before you spend any money.

To name the problem, answer three questions:

  1. Who will use the AI's answer?
  2. What will that person decide with it?
  3. When do they make that decision?

Then they put the three answers into one sentence. If the team cannot agree on the answers, they do not yet know what problem they are solving. They settle that first.

Weak

"Use AI to improve aircraft maintenance."

It answers none of the three questions. Nobody can tell what "improve" means, so nobody can tell whether the project worked.

Strong

"The AI will help the maintenance chief decide which parts to inspect early, each morning before the flight schedule is set."

  • Who: the maintenance chief
  • What: which parts to inspect early
  • When: each morning, before the flight schedule is set

Section 2 · Two practices in every success

Pair builders with experts. Start small.

Pair builders with domain experts

The people who build an AI system are usually data scientists. They know how to train a model. They usually do not know the work the model is for, such as how a crew maintains aircraft or how a clinic schedules patients. A domain expert is someone who does that work every day.

The domain expert knows which data can be trusted. A box on a maintenance form may look perfect for training. If mechanics fill it in quickly at the end of a shift, the data scientist sees clean numbers. The mechanic knows the numbers are guesses.

On the flight line, that person is a senior maintainer. At the clinic, the front desk staff. At the school, the reading teacher. At the bakery, the baker.

Start small, then scale

A pilot tests the system on a small scale before the whole organization pays for it. If the system does not work, the organization finds out after spending a little, on one schedule for two months, instead of a lot, across every clinic. A pilot that shows the system does not work is still a useful result. It stops a bad system before it reaches everyone.

A pilot has four parts:

  1. A limited test
  2. Real users
  3. A clear success measure
  4. A set end date

What does a wrong prediction cost?

Your first question is not "Which vendor?" It is "What does a wrong prediction cost?"

Every prediction system makes two kinds of mistakes, and they almost never cost the same.

Section 3 · Two kinds of mistakes

Misses and false alarms

A prediction system says yes or no: this part will fail, this patient will miss, this student is falling behind. Reality also says yes or no. That makes four outcomes.

Flight lineA failing part stays on the aircraft. A mission can be grounded, or worse.A good part is pulled and replaced. It costs money and time.

Section 3 · Cost lab

Find the cheapest alert setting

A model gives every part, or every appointment, a risk score from 0 to 100. Someone must choose the cutoff: anything with a score at or above it gets flagged. A low cutoff flags more, so it catches more real problems and raises more false alarms. A high cutoff does the opposite.

Section 4

Implementation is a cycle

Launch is not the finish line. It is the start of monitoring.

An implementation framework is a structured plan for taking an AI idea from a problem to a working system. You met CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) and the AI Canvas in Unit 1, and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework in Unit 2. Every good framework forces the same questions.

Section 4 · The lifecycle

Six phases that loop

PhaseWhat happensWhere projects break
1. Define the problemName the decision, the users, the success measure, and the cost of each error.Skipped or rushed. Root cause 1.
2. Assess the dataFind the data, check its quality, and confirm you are allowed to use it.Data turns out to be missing, messy, or off-limits.
3. Build or buyChoose a pre-built service, customize one, or build your own model.The tool is chosen before the problem is clear.
4. TestCheck accuracy on data the model has not seen, and check results for each group of people affected.Only overall accuracy is reported. Weak spots stay hidden.
5. DeployPut predictions in front of the people who act on them, with training and a human check.The output lands on a screen nobody looks at.
6. Monitor and improveWatch accuracy over time, catch drift, retrain, or retire the system.Nobody owns the system after launch.
Decision gates. Well-run projects put a gate at the end of the key phases: a planned point where someone with authority decides to continue, change course, or stop. Gates make stopping a normal, planned outcome instead of an embarrassing failure.

Section 4 · Drift

The bakery's model stops working

The bakery's morning pastry forecast worked well for a year. The baker bakes what the forecast says. In month 13, a school opens next door. Parents start buying pastries on the way to drop-off. The model learned from a year of sales before the school existed, so it keeps predicting the old pattern. Every pastry the forecast leaves out is a customer who finds an empty shelf.

Monitoring means the system checks its own forecast against what actually sold, every day. If the error goes above 15 percent, it sends the owner an alert. The owner can then retrain the model on the new sales.

What you do: Run the bakery twice. First, with Run 1: No monitoring selected, select Add a month until you reach month 18. Then select Run 2: Monitoring on and do the same. Compare the red bars on the chart and the two rows of the table.
Section 4 · Who is responsible

Every phase needs a person in charge

Each phase of the lifecycle needs a specific person who is responsible for getting it done. "The team will handle it" is not enough. When no one is responsible for a job, nobody does it. An AI project usually has these five roles.

RoleWhat they doAt the clinic
Project sponsorFunds the work and makes the gate decisions.The clinic director
Domain expertsKnow the work the AI supports and which data can be trusted.Front desk staff and a nurse
Data specialistsFind, clean, and prepare the data.The clinic's records analyst
BuildersTrain or configure the model.The vendor's team
Operations ownerRuns and monitors the system after launch.The front desk supervisor

In a small organization like the bakery, one person may fill several roles. The role most often left empty is the operations owner, the person who watches the system after launch (phase 6). Once the system is running, everyone moves on to other work. If nobody has the job of checking it, the system can get worse for months before anyone notices, the way the bakery's forecast did.

Section 5 · Inference timing

Batch or real time?

Batch

The model makes many predictions at once on a schedule, such as overnight. The bakery's forecast runs at 2 a.m. and is ready when the baker arrives at 4 a.m. Cheaper and simpler to run.

Real time

The model answers each request the moment it arrives. A credit card fraud check has to answer in a fraction of a second while you stand at the register. Costs more and is harder to run.

Do not pay for speed you do not need. Ask how soon a person must act on the prediction.

Section 5 · Monitoring

What monitoring watches

SignalWhat it tells you
Accuracy over timeWhether predictions still match what actually happens. A slow decline is drift.
Changes in the input dataWhether today's data looks different from the training data. An early warning of drift.
Results by groupWhether errors fall more heavily on one group of people. The bias problem from Unit 2.
OverridesHow often people ignore or reverse the prediction. A rising rate means users have stopped trusting it, often for good reason.
Cost and speedWhether the system is getting slower or more expensive as use grows.

Common data problems in the pipeline

  • Missing values: half the clinic's records have no phone number, so reminder texts cannot be sent.
  • Inconsistent entries: the flight line logs the same part as "hyd pump," "hydraulic pump," and "HP-2."
  • Unreliable entries: a box on a form that looks useful was filled in carelessly because nobody ever checked it.
  • Old data: the bakery's data is all from before the office building opened across the street.
  • Data you cannot legally use: student records collected for grading may not be approved for a prediction system.

Section 6 · Two kinds of cloud AI service

Send one photo to both services

Every API call has three parts. The API key proves who is asking and which account gets the bill. The request carries your photo and what you want done with it. The response comes back in the same layout every time, usually JSON, so a program knows where to find each part.

Amazon Rekognition: a task API

It does one job. It finds every line of text in the photo and returns each one with a confidence score from 0 to 100. You cannot tell it which text you care about.

Gemini: a GenAI model API

It follows a written instruction, the prompt, and writes an answer. It reports how many tokens it used, which sets the price. It reports no confidence score.

The scoring rule from your project. A read is correct only if every letter, number, and symbol matches the answer key. Capital letters and extra spaces don't count. Everything else does.

Section 6 · What renting costs besides money

  
Your data leaves your control

  

   
Data sovereignty
When you use a cloud service, your data may be processed in another country. Laws about personal data differ from country to country, and some data, such as health records, student records, and government information, must stay in approved locations. That matters for anyone living and working overseas.

   
Vendor lock-in
Once your systems, data, and staff skills depend on one provider, switching gets slow and expensive. The vendor can raise prices, change the model, or retire the service, and you have little choice but to adjust.

Section 6 · Comparing platforms

  
The weighted decision matrix

  
A weighted decision matrix turns a comparison of several criteria into a choice you can defend. First, remove any platform that fails a must-have, such as a required compliance approval. Then weight the remaining criteria by how much they matter, score each platform from 1 to 5, multiply, and add.

  
The clinic confirmed that both platforms will sign a BAA. Now it compares them.

Section 6 · Build

  
Buy, customize, or build

  
No vendor has trained a model on your squadron's maintenance history, your clinic's appointment book, or your bakery's sales. For a problem that specific, you need a development platform: a cloud environment where you prepare your own data, train a model, and put it to work.

  

   
ApproachWhat it meansBest whenWatch out for

   
BuyUse a pre-built service as is.The task is common: reading text, labeling images, transcribing speech.It cannot learn your situation. Your data leaves your control.

   
CustomizeStart with a pre-built service and train it further on your own examples.The task is common but your data is special, such as your own part types.You need good labeled examples. Some lock-in remains.

   
BuildTrain your own model on a development platform.The problem is specific to your organization and the data is yours.Takes time, skills, and ongoing maintenance. You own every failure.

  

  

   
Development platforms
  • Amazon: SageMaker AI
  • Microsoft: Azure Machine Learning, within Microsoft Foundry
  • Google: Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (called Vertex AI until 2026)

   
AutoML and its tradeoff
AutoML (automated machine learning) tries many candidate models and returns the best one, so a small team can build a working model in days. The tradeoff: fast to build, hard to explain. If your model grounds an aircraft or flags a seven-year-old as a struggling reader, someone will ask why.
